The challenge
“The underlying physical laws necessary for the mathematical theory of a large part of physics and the whole of chemistry are thus completely known, and the difficulty is only that the exact application of these laws leads to equations much too complicated to be soluble. It therefore becomes desirable that approximate practical methods of applying quantum mechanics should be developed, which can lead to an explanation of the main features of complex atomic systems without too much computation.”
P. A. M. Dirac, Quantum Mechanics of Many-Electron Systems, Proc. R. Soc. A 123, 714 (1929)

Overview

The story in six lines

1
One equation, no exact solutions
The electronic Schrödinger equation is known exactly. Solving it exactly costs exponentially in the number of electrons.
2
Two ways to approximate
Approximate the wavefunction (Hartree–Fock, then perturbation theory, CI, coupled clusters), or replace it by the density (DFT).
3
The wavefunction ladder converges, slowly and expensively
CCSD(T) in a big basis reaches 1 kcal/mol for small molecules, at a cost that grows like $N^7$.
4
Jacob's ladder trades cost for accuracy, without a guarantee
Each rung adds an ingredient: density, gradient, kinetic energy density, exact exchange, unoccupied orbitals. Higher is usually better, not always.
5
The failures are systematic
Self-interaction, static correlation and dispersion explain most of the big errors; each fix tends to break something else.
6
Machine learning changes the economics
Learned potentials, functionals and wavefunctions move top-rung accuracy toward bottom-rung cost. The labels still come from the ladder.

Here is the whole talk in six lines, so you can follow where we are.

One: the equation is known; solving it is exponential. Two: two strategies, approximate the wavefunction or replace it by the density. Three: the wavefunction ladder converges systematically, and CCSD(T) in a big basis gets about 1 kcal/mol for small molecules, but its cost grows like N to the seventh. Four: Jacob's ladder of density functionals trades cost for accuracy by adding ingredients, without any guarantee. Five: the big DFT failures come from a small number of systematic errors. Overview · accuracy

The yardstick: 1 kcal/mol out of 70,000

Pople, Nobel lecture 1998: "a global accuracy of 1 kcal/mole would be appropriate". At room temperature an error of 1.36 kcal/mol is a factor of 10 in a rate or a binding constant.
Chemistry lives in differences of total energies, at a relative precision of about 10⁻⁵. Approximations survive only when their errors cancel between reactants and products.
N₂: total energy −109.54 Eh, Hartree–Fock limit −108.99 Eh, De 228.5 kcal/mol (W4-11) · 1 Eh = 627.5 kcal/mol = 27.21 eV

Why is this hard? Because of the scale. Take N₂. Its total energy is about 69,000 kcal/mol. (click) The correlation energy, the part Hartree–Fock misses, is 344 kcal/mol, and the energy to break the triple bond is 228. (click) A typical barrier is 20, and chemistry cares about 1 kcal/mol.

So we need relative accuracy around 10⁻⁵ on total energies, which nobody achieves directly. What saves us is that errors cancel between reactants and products, and every method in this talk lives or dies by how well its errors cancel.

The problem

One equation for all of chemistry

$\hat H\,\Psi(\mathbf r_1,\dots,\mathbf r_N)=E\,\Psi,\qquad \hat H=\underbrace{-\tfrac12\sum_i\nabla_i^2}_{\text{kinetic}}\;\underbrace{-\sum_{i,A}\frac{Z_A}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf R_A|}}_{\text{nuclei}}\;+\underbrace{\sum_{i\lt j}\frac{1}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|}}_{\text{electron–electron}}$
  • Born–Oppenheimer (1927): nuclei are 1,836× heavier, so solve for electrons at fixed nuclei. The energy $E(\mathbf R)$ is the potential energy surface: structures, barriers, vibrations.
  • Antisymmetry: $\Psi$ changes sign when two electrons swap (Pauli).
  • The last term couples everything. Without it the problem separates into one-electron problems and is easy.

The problem

The exponential wall

$\Psi$ depends on $3N$ coordinates. Tabulating it, even crudely, takes $p^{3N}$ numbers.
Hartree (1948): a wavefunction of 78 variables "would require 1078 entries".

Kohn (Nobel lecture, 1999): beyond about 10 atoms, wavefunction methods hit an exponential wall; for $N\gtrsim10^3$ the wavefunction "is not a legitimate scientific concept".
Exact methods (full CI) in a finite basis grow just as fast. The 2024 record is 1.3 × 10¹² determinants: propane in the smallest basis.
So every practical method is an approximation that throws away most of $\Psi$. Which part to throw away is the history of the field.

Why not just solve it numerically? The wavefunction has 3N coordinates. Even with three grid points per coordinate you need 3 to the 3N numbers. That's the red band. (click) Hartree said this in 1948: 78 variables would need 10 to the 78 entries. Kohn's Nobel lecture called it the exponential wall: beyond about ten atoms, wavefunction methods at chemical accuracy hit it, and for a thousand electrons the wavefunction "is not a legitimate scientific concept".

(click) Working in a basis doesn't save you: full CI, the exact answer in a basis, grows just as fast. The record, in 2024, is 1.3 trillion determinants, for propane in the smallest basis there is. The universe has 10⁸⁰ atoms; caffeine's full CI would need far more numbers than that.

History

A century of approximations

Dates of the first publication; see the blog for sources

Let the timeline run. Four lanes: wavefunction methods, density functionals, basis sets and codes, and machine learning.

The 1920s and 30s: Schrödinger, Heitler and London's H₂ bond, Hartree's self-consistent field, Hylleraas's helium, Fock and Slater's antisymmetrization, Møller–Plesset. Thomas–Fermi and Dirac's exchange started the density lane at the same time.

The 1950s: Boys's Gaussians and Roothaan's matrix formulation make computation possible. The 1960s: Hohenberg–Kohn and Kohn–Sham, and Čížek's coupled clusters. Then Pople's Gaussian program, and through the 80s the functionals that work for molecules, B88 and LYP, then hybrids. CCSD(T) appears in 1989. The 1998 Nobel prize went to Kohn and Pople.

The ML lane starts in 2007 with Behler–Parrinello potentials and arrives at learned functionals and huge datasets in 2021–2025.

Part I · Hartree–Fock

Hartree–Fock: each electron in the average field of the others

Best single Slater determinant: antisymmetric by construction, so exchange is exact; correlation is absent.
$\hat f\,\phi_i=\varepsilon_i\,\phi_i,\qquad \hat f=-\tfrac12\nabla^2+v_{\rm nuc}+\hat J-\hat K$
$\hat f$ depends on the orbitals it produces, so it is solved by iteration: guess orbitals, build the field, re-solve, repeat. Hartree (1928): "a process of successive approximation".
Roothaan and Hall (1951) expand orbitals in a basis: $\mathbf{FC}=\mathbf{SC}\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, a matrix problem. Cost $\propto N^4$ (two-electron integrals).
Self-consistent-field iterations of the atom engine in this page (DIIS extrapolation): about 10–20 cycles to µEh.
Hartree–Fock gets 99.5% of the total energy right. The missing 0.5% is the correlation energy, and it is most of chemistry.

Hartree–Fock is the best single Slater determinant. Because a determinant is antisymmetric, exchange is treated exactly. What's missing is correlation: each electron sees only the average of the others.

The Fock operator depends on its own solutions, so we iterate: guess orbitals, build the field, solve, repeat until nothing changes. Hartree called it "a process of successive approximation" in 1928. On the right are real convergence histories from the engine in this page: ten to twenty iterations to microhartree, with DIIS extrapolation.

(click) Roothaan and Hall turned it into a matrix problem in a basis, FC = SCε. The cost is dominated by the two-electron integrals, N⁴.

(click) And the punchline: Hartree–Fock gets about 99.5% of the total energy. The missing half percent is most of chemistry.

Part I · basis sets

Basis sets: Gaussians are wrong in shape, right in cost

  • Atomic orbitals look like $e^{-\zeta r}$ (Slater): a cusp at the nucleus, an exponential tail.
  • Boys (1950): use Gaussians $e^{-\alpha r^2}$. Their products are Gaussians, so every integral is analytic. Fix the shape by contracting several (STO-3G: three per orbital).
  • Names to decode: 6-31G* (Pople: split valence + polarization), cc-pVXZ (Dunning 1989: correlation-consistent, X = D, T, Q, 5 → extrapolate), def2-TZVP (Ahlrichs).
Best fits of a Slater 1s orbital by 1, 3 and 6 Gaussians, recomputed here (the 3-Gaussian fit reproduces Hehre, Stewart & Pople's 1969 STO-3G exponents to six digits).
Correlation energy converges slowly with basis size (error ∝ X⁻³), because no smooth basis captures the electron–electron cusp.

The orbitals have to be expanded in something. Real atomic orbitals look like e to the minus r, Slater functions, with a cusp at the nucleus and an exponential tail.

(click) Boys's insight in 1950: use Gaussians instead. The product of two Gaussians on different centres is a Gaussian, so every integral is analytic. The shape is wrong, so you contract several: one Gaussian is poor; three, the famous STO-3G, is decent; six is close, except right at the cusp, in the inset. These fits are recomputed here, and the three-Gaussian fit reproduces Pople's 1969 exponents to six digits.

(click) The names you'll see: 6-31G*, Pople's split-valence sets; cc-pVXZ, Dunning's correlation-consistent sets, designed so you can extrapolate in X; def2 from Ahlrichs. The correlation energy converges slowly, as X to the minus three, because no smooth basis captures the electron–electron cusp. That's why explicitly correlated F12 methods exist.

Part I · correlation

99.5% of the energy, about half of each bond

Löwdin (1959): the correlation energy is $E_{\rm exact}-E_{\rm HF}$.
Helium: $E_{\rm HF}=-2.86168$, exact $-2.90372\ E_h$. The difference, 42 mEh = 26 kcal/mol, is only 1.4% of the energy.
But bonds are made of differences. Hartree–Fock recovers 67% of water's atomization energy and 50% of N₂'s, and it does not bind F₂ or O₃ at all.
Electrons avoid each other more than a mean field allows: each carries a Coulomb hole. Correlation energy is comparable to bond energies.

Part I · static correlation

Breaking H₂: one determinant is not enough

Computed live in this page: STO-3G basis, restricted and unrestricted Hartree–Fock and full CI (two configurations). Matches Szabo & Ostlund: −1.1167 and −1.1373 Eh at 1.4 bohr.
Static correlation: near-degenerate configurations. RHF keeps ionic terms forever; UHF gets the energy by breaking spin symmetry (⟨S²⟩ → 1); only a second configuration fixes both.

The simplest place to see what goes wrong: H₂ in the smallest basis, computed live. The grey curve is restricted Hartree–Fock. Near equilibrium it's fine. Stretch the bond and it climbs far above two hydrogen atoms.

The bars on the right explain why. A single doubly occupied bonding orbital puts both electrons on the same atom, H⁺H⁻, half of the time, at every distance. At dissociation that's absurd.

(click) Full CI here is just two configurations, bonding squared and antibonding squared. The second configuration enters with a negative sign and cancels the ionic terms, so the ionic weight goes to zero as the bond breaks, and the energy goes to two atoms.

(click) Unrestricted Hartree–Fock gets the energy by putting the up electron on one atom and the down electron on the other, at the cost of breaking spin symmetry. This is static correlation, and we'll see it again in DFT.

Part I · coupled clusters

Truncated CI is not size consistent; coupled clusters are

CI: $\Psi=c_0\Phi_0+\sum c_i^a\Phi_i^a+\sum c_{ij}^{ab}\Phi_{ij}^{ab}+\dots$ Exact if all excitations are kept (full CI), variational, but factorial.
Truncate at doubles (CISD) and a test fails: $N$ non-interacting molecules should have $N$ times the energy of one.
Coupled cluster (Čížek 1966)
$\Psi=e^{\hat T}\Phi_0,\ \hat T=\hat T_1+\hat T_2+\dots$
The exponential contains products of excitations ($\tfrac12\hat T_2^2$: quadruples), so separate fragments stay separate. CCSD is exact for any number of separated two-electron systems.
Size consistency is why coupled clusters, not truncated CI, became the standard.

Configuration interaction writes Ψ as the reference plus all singly, doubly, ... excited determinants. Keep all of them and you have full CI: exact in the basis, factorial in cost. So you truncate, usually at doubles.

(click) And truncation fails a simple test. Take N hydrogen molecules far apart. The exact correlation energy is N times one molecule's. CISD only allows two excitations in the whole system, so it can't correlate all molecules at once. With this analytic model built from the live H₂ calculation, CISD recovers about 92% per molecule at N = 8, 58% at N = 100, and it tends to zero for large N.

(click) Coupled cluster fixes this with an exponential: e to the T contains products of excitations, so separate fragments stay separate. For separated two-electron systems CCSD is exact for any N. That property, size consistency, is why coupled clusters became the standard.

Part II · foundations

The density is enough, in principle

$n(\mathbf r)$ has 3 variables for any number of electrons. Thomas and Fermi (1927) tried energy as a functional of $n$ alone: no shells, and no molecule binds (Teller 1962).
Hohenberg–Kohn (1964)
1. The ground-state density determines the external potential, hence everything.
2. A universal functional $F[n]$ exists, and $E[n]=F[n]+\int v\,n$ is minimized by the true density.
The catch: the theorem proves $F[n]$ exists and says nothing about its form. Finding it efficiently for all systems is QMA-hard (Schuch & Verstraete 2009).
Proof idea, in four lines
Suppose two potentials $v\neq v'$ give the same density $n$. Use each ground state as a trial state for the other Hamiltonian:
$E\lt E'+\int(v-v')\,n,\qquad E'\lt E+\int(v'-v)\,n$
Adding them gives $E+E'\lt E+E'$, a contradiction. So $n\mapsto v$ is one-to-one.
Density functional theory is exact and almost useless, until Kohn and Sham's trick a year later.

The density depends on three variables no matter how many electrons you have. Thomas and Fermi tried this in 1927: energy as a functional of density alone. It gives no shell structure, and Teller proved that no molecule binds.

(click) Hohenberg and Kohn, 1964: the ground-state density determines the external potential, so it determines everything. The proof is four lines, on the right: assume two potentials give the same density, use each ground state as a trial function for the other, add the inequalities and get a contradiction.

(click) The catch: the universal functional exists, but the theorem says nothing about its form, and computing it efficiently for all systems is QMA-hard. (click) So DFT in 1964 was exact and almost useless.

Part II · Kohn–Sham

Kohn–Sham (1965): put the unknown where it is small

Use non-interacting electrons that have the same density. Their kinetic energy $T_s$ is computed exactly from orbitals; only one piece is unknown:
$E[n]=T_s[n]+\int v\,n+\underbrace{\tfrac12\!\iint\!\frac{n(\mathbf r)n(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}}_{J[n]}+E_{\rm xc}[n]$
$E_{\rm xc}$ collects exchange, correlation, and the kinetic-energy correction. Self-consistent orbitals as in Hartree–Fock, at the same or lower cost.
$E_{\rm xc}$ is 10% of the energy but larger than any bond energy. Everything below is about approximating it.

Part II · LDA

Rung 1, LDA: borrow the uniform electron gas

$E_{\rm xc}^{\rm LDA}=\int n\,\varepsilon_{\rm xc}^{\rm unif}(n)\,d\mathbf r,\qquad \varepsilon_{\rm x}^{\rm unif}=-\tfrac34\left(\tfrac3\pi\right)^{1/3}n^{1/3}$
  • Exchange: exact for the gas (Dirac 1930). Correlation: Ceperley & Alder's quantum Monte Carlo (1980), fitted by VWN (1980) and PW92.
  • It works better than it should: the LDA exchange-correlation hole is that of a real system, so it obeys the sum rule (Gunnarsson & Lundqvist 1976).
  • Good geometries and solids; for molecules it overbinds every molecule: G2/97 atomization energies, MAE 82.9 kcal/mol, mean error +82.9.

Part II · GGA

Rung 2, GGA: add the gradient

$E_{\rm x}^{\rm GGA}=\int n\,\varepsilon_{\rm x}^{\rm unif}(n)\,F_{\rm x}(s)\,d\mathbf r,\qquad s=\dfrac{|\nabla n|}{2k_Fn}$
The naive gradient expansion makes things worse. GGAs choose $F_{\rm x}(s)$ to fix it.
B88 (Becke): correct $-1/r$ energy density far out, one parameter $\beta$ fitted to exact exchange energies of the noble gases.

Part II · meta-GGA

Rung 3, meta-GGA: recognize what kind of region you are in

Add the kinetic energy density $\tau=\tfrac12\sum_i|\nabla\phi_i|^2$. The combination
$\alpha=\dfrac{\tau-\tau_W}{\tau_{\rm unif}},\qquad \tau_W=\dfrac{|\nabla n|^2}{8n}$
tells single-orbital regions ($\alpha=0$: covalent bonds, H atoms) from slowly varying ones ($\alpha\approx1$) and weak overlaps ($\alpha\gg1$).
SCAN (Sun, Ruzsinszky, Perdew 2015): "obeying all 17 known exact constraints that a meta-GGA can". r²SCAN (2020) restores numerical stability. M06-L and B97M-V take the fitting route.

Meta-GGAs add the kinetic energy density tau. The useful combination is alpha, which compares tau with its single-orbital limit and with the uniform gas.

Part III · delocalization

LDA and PBE are convex: they reward spreading (delocalization error), the many-electron face of self-interaction.
B3LYP's 20% exact exchange helps only a little: global hybrids reduce the error, they do not remove it.

(click) Third, learn the wavefunction itself, variational Monte Carlo with a neural-network ansatz. No labels needed: the variational principle is the loss.

(click) The first two inherit the ladder through their data. The third climbs the wavefunction ladder directly, at a high price.

Takeaways

Five things to remember

1
Every method is an approximation to one equation.
Dirac said so in 1929; the century since is about which part of Ψ to throw away.
2
The wavefunction ladder is systematic.
HF → MP2 → CCSD → CCSD(T) converges toward exact, at N⁴ … N⁷ cost, and fails when one determinant is not enough.
3
Jacob's ladder is pragmatic.
Density → gradient → τ → exact exchange → unoccupied orbitals: usually better and costlier, never guaranteed.
4
Errors have physics.
Self-interaction, static correlation and missing dispersion explain the large failures; fixes for one often worsen another.
5
ML changes the price, not the target.
Learned functionals and potentials carry top-rung accuracy toward low-rung cost; the labels and the constraints decide how far.
