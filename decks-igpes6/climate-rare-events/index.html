How did people start thinking about this? I find it useful to see five eras, each defined by a new idea about where the tail comes from.

First, statistics: fit the tail of the observed record and extrapolate. [Build 1] Second, chaos: Lorenz showed that weather is unpredictable beyond about two weeks, so forecasts became probability distributions computed by ensembles. [Build 2] Third, attribution: if we can simulate the weather many times, we can simulate many worlds, with and without human influence, and ask how the odds of an event changed. [Build 3] Fourth, rare-event algorithms from statistical physics, which steer simulations into the tail instead of waiting for it. [Build 4] And fifth, AI emulators, which make simulation thousands of times cheaper.

[Build 5] Each era added a tool, and none replaced the previous one. The best answers today combine all five.

History · era 1 · statistics of extremes

Era 1: fit the tail of the data, then extrapolate

Maxima of long blocks (a year, a summer) have only three possible limit laws (Fisher & Tippett 1928, Gnedenko 1943), unified as the generalized extreme value law:
$G(z)=\exp\!\Big\{-\big[1+\xi\,\tfrac{z-\mu}{\sigma}\big]^{-1/\xi}\Big\}$
The return level $z_R$ solves $G(z_R)=1-1/R$: the level exceeded on average once every $R$ years (Gumbel).
It became engineering law: after the 1953 North Sea flood, the Netherlands set dikes for central Holland to a 1-in-10,000-year water level.
But the shape parameter $\xi$, which decides the far tail, is the hardest thing to estimate from 70 years.
Fisher & Tippett 1928 · Gnedenko 1943 · Jenkinson 1955 · Gumbel 1958 · Pickands 1975 · Coles 2001 · demo: synthetic "hottest day of the year" with a known GEV law ($\xi=-0.2$), L-moment fits, 90% bootstrap bands

The oldest approach is purely statistical. Fisher and Tippett in 1928 and Gnedenko in 1943 showed that maxima of long blocks, like the hottest day of each year, can only follow three limit laws, which today we write as one: the generalized extreme value distribution. Gumbel turned this into the language of return periods.

[Animation] Here is a synthetic record: 70 years of the hottest day of the year, drawn from a known law. [Build 1] We fit the extreme value law with its uncertainty band: it is tight within the record.

[Build 2] Now extrapolate to 10,000 years, the kind of number used for design. This became engineering law: after the 1953 North Sea flood, the Netherlands designed the dikes of central Holland for a 1-in-10,000-year water level. Look at the band: from 70 years, the 1-in-1000-year level is uncertain by several degrees.

[Build 3] And here are other 70-year records from the same climate. The fitted tail swings around, and for temperature the fit often implies an upper bound that is too low. That is exactly what happened before the Pacific Northwest heatwave of 2021: the event was beyond what a fit to the previous record allowed.

History · era 2 · chaos and ensembles

Era 2: chaos turns every forecast into a probability

Lorenz 1963: a three-variable convection model is deterministic, yet tiny errors grow exponentially. Lorenz 1969: for the atmosphere the limit is a few days for weather systems and at most a few weeks for the largest scales.
Epstein 1969, Leith 1974: so forecast the whole distribution, with an ensemble of perturbed runs. Operational at ECMWF and the US NMC since 1992.
The two lineages share a birthplace: ENIAC ran the first computerised Monte Carlo in 1948 and the first numerical weather forecast in 1950.
Hasselmann 1976: fast weather acts as noise on the slow climate. Climate models became stochastic dynamical systems, and their rare excursions became questions of large deviations (Freidlin & Wentzell).
An extreme is now a tail probability of a simulated ensemble, not only of the record.

The second era starts with Lorenz in 1963: a three-variable model of convection that is completely deterministic, yet tiny errors grow exponentially. [Build 1] Epstein in 1969 and Leith in 1974 drew the conclusion: forecast the whole probability distribution, by running an ensemble from perturbed initial conditions. [Build 3] From here on, an extreme is a tail probability of a simulated ensemble, not only of the observed record.

History · era 3 · attribution and large ensembles

Era 3: if we can simulate weather, we can simulate many worlds

"Will it ever be possible to sue anyone for damaging the climate?"Myles Allen, Nature, 2003
Stott, Stone & Allen 2004: whether the 2003 heatwave was caused by us is "ill-posed", but we can estimate how much we raised its risk: at least doubled, with more than 90% confidence.

$\mathrm{PR}=\dfrac{p_1}{p_0},\qquad \mathrm{FAR}=1-\dfrac{p_0}{p_1}$
Attribution turned an unanswerable question into a ratio of two tail probabilities, which is exactly what needs sampling.

The third era starts with a provocative question. In 2003 Myles Allen asked in Nature: will it ever be possible to sue anyone for damaging the climate?

[Build 1] A year later Stott, Stone and Allen gave the template answer for the 2003 heatwave. Asking whether we caused it is ill-posed: almost any weather event could have happened by chance. But we can ask how much we raised its risk: at least doubled, with more than 90 percent confidence.

[Build 2] And that requires the probability of the same event in two climates: with and without human influence. The ratio of the two tail probabilities is the probability ratio; one minus its inverse is the fraction of attributable risk. Computing them needed huge ensembles: volunteer computing with climateprediction.net, which had 25,000 users on its first weekend; then large initial-condition ensembles like Japan's d4PDF with more than five thousand simulated years per climate; and UNSEEN, which reuses seasonal forecasts as extra years of weather.

[Build 3] So attribution turned an unanswerable question into a ratio of two tail probabilities, which is exactly what needs sampling.

The setting · what we compute

The setting: one model, one observable, four questions

A model of the climate
$X_{t+\delta t}=F(X_t)+\text{noise}$
a climate model (GCM), a weather model, or an AI emulator; the state $X_t$ holds $10^{6}$ to $10^{9}$ numbers
An observable and an event
$A=\dfrac1T\displaystyle\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}\! a(X_t)\,\dd t,\qquad E=\{A>a\}$
7-day mean temperature over France · a 90-day summer mean · 3-day rainfall over a river basin · the strength of the Atlantic overturning
How rare is it?
$p(a)=\mathbb P(A>a)$, return period $r(a)\approx\dfrac{\Delta}{p(a)}$
one block of length $\Delta$ (a summer, a year) per sample
What does it look like?
$\mathbb E\,[\,\cdot\mid A>a\,]$
composites, mechanisms, storylines of the event
Can we see it coming?
$q(x)=\mathbb P(A>a\mid X_t=x)$
the committor, which is exactly a probabilistic forecast
Did warming change it?
$\mathrm{PR}=\dfrac{p_{\,\rm today}(a)}{p_{\,\rm pre\text{-}industrial}(a)}$
attribution; for tipping points: transition rates
All four are expectations over whole trajectories of the model, and all four live in the tail.

Let me set up the problem the way a statistician or a physicist would. We have a model of the climate: a state X_t, which for a climate model is a million to a billion numbers, and a rule that moves it forward in time, with some noise or chaos. It can be a physics-based climate model, a weather model, or an AI emulator.

We pick an observable: for example the 7-day mean temperature over France, or a 90-day summer mean, or 3-day rainfall over a river basin. An extreme event is the observable exceeding a high threshold.

[Build 1] Then there are four questions. First, how rare is it: the probability, which we usually report as a return period, the average waiting time between events. [Build 2] Second, what does it look like: the conditional distribution of the whole atmosphere given that the event happens: composite maps, mechanisms, storylines. [Build 3] Third, can we see it coming: the probability of the event given today's state. Physicists call this the committor; meteorologists call it a probabilistic forecast. It is the same object. [Build 4] Fourth, did warming change it: the ratio of probabilities in today's climate and in a pre-industrial climate. For tipping points, the analogue is a transition rate.

[Build 5] All four questions are expectations over whole trajectories of the model, and all four live in the tail of the distribution.

The setting · why it is hard

Brute force is hopeless in the tail

Count how often the event happens in $N$ independent simulated years:
$\hat p=\dfrac{\#\{A_n>a\}}{N},\qquad \dfrac{\mathrm{sd}(\hat p)}{p}=\sqrt{\dfrac{1-p}{Np}}$
For 10% error we need $N\approx 100/p$ years, for every climate we care about.

The obvious approach: simulate many years and count. The relative error of that count is the square root of one over N p. For a 10% error you need about 100 over p samples.

[Build 1] So a 1-in-100-year event needs ten thousand simulated years, and a 1-in-1000-year event needs a hundred thousand, and you need that again for every climate you care about: today, plus 1.5 degrees, plus 2 degrees.

[Build 2] Observations do not save us: about 80 years of reliable global records, in a climate that is changing underneath us.

The setting · a unifying view

One principle behind every method: tilt, then reweight

Simulate under a law $Q$ that visits the tail, then correct exactly:
$\mathbb E_P[O]=\mathbb E_Q\!\Big[\,O\,\dfrac{\dd P}{\dd Q}\Big]$
The best tilt is the event itself, $Q^\star=P(\,\cdot\mid E)$: zero variance. On path space it is a Doob transform, an extra drift
$u^\star_t(x)=\sigma\sigma^{\!\top}\nabla\log h_t(x),\qquad h_t(x)=\mathbb P(E\mid X_t=x)$
$h$ is the committor, the forecast probability of the event. We never know it; every method approximates it.
methodhow it approximates $h$
cloning (GKTL)$e^{\,k\int A\,\dd t}$, applied by a population that clones and kills
splitting (AMS, TAMS)the levels of a score function $\xi(x)$
ensemble boostingrestart near the precursors of a known extreme
AI-boosted samplingan AI emulator's forecast of the event
control, neural samplerslearn the drift $\nabla\log h$ directly
Rare-event sampling is learning the forecast $h$ well enough to steer by it, and reweighting what is left.

Here is the principle behind every method in this talk, and it is the same principle as in my other work on samplers. Simulate under a different law Q that visits the tail more often, and correct exactly with the likelihood ratio.

[Build 1] The best possible Q is the law conditioned on the event. That has zero variance. On path space it has a beautiful form: it is a Doob transform, the original dynamics plus an extra drift, the gradient of log h, where h is the probability of the event given the current state: the committor, the forecast. Of course we never know h. If we knew it we would already have the answer.

[Build 2] And every method is a different way to approximate h. Cloning uses an exponential of the accumulated observable, applied by a population. Splitting uses the levels of a score function. Ensemble boosting restarts near the precursors of a known event. AI-boosted sampling uses an AI emulator's forecast. And control-based methods and neural samplers try to learn the drift directly.

[Build 3] So one way to say it: rare-event sampling is learning the forecast well enough to steer by it, and reweighting what is left over.

Methods · cloning

Cloning: let a population find the heatwave

1
Run $N$ copies of the model.
2
Every $\tau$ days, weight each copy by the heat it just produced: $W_n=\exp\!\big(k\!\int_{t-\tau}^{t}A(X_n)\,\dd s\big)$.
3
Clone the heavy copies, kill the light ones, keep $N$. Clones of a deterministic model get a tiny perturbation so they diverge.
4
The population samples the tilted law
$\dfrac{\dd Q_k}{\dd P}=\dfrac{1}{Z(k)}\,e^{\,k\int_0^T\! A\,\dd t}$
and we undo the tilt exactly:
$\hat p(a)=\dfrac{\hat Z}{N}\displaystyle\sum_n \mathbb 1[A_n>a]\,e^{-kTA_n}$
$\hat Z=\prod_i \overline{W}_i$, an unbiased estimate of $Z(k)$
Giardinà, Kurchan & Peliti, PRL 2006 · Tailleur & Kurchan, Nat. Phys. 2007 · Ragone, Wouters & Bouchet, PNAS 2018 · toy: daily AR(1) temperature anomalies, 30-day season

The first family is cloning, also called the GKTL algorithm after Giardinà, Kurchan, Tailleur and Lecomte, who developed it to compute large deviations in statistical physics. Ragone, Wouters and Bouchet brought it into a climate model in 2018.

[Animation] This is a toy summer: daily temperature anomalies with a few days of memory. Forty independent summers. On the right, each dot is the season mean so far, with the 1-in-100 and 1-in-1000-year levels. Brute force: nothing gets close.

[Build 1] Now cloning. Every three days, each copy gets a weight: the exponential of the heat it produced in the last three days. Heavy copies are cloned, light copies are killed, and we keep the number fixed. Watch the gray lines disappear and the hot lines branch. In a deterministic model the clones get a tiny perturbation so that they diverge.

[Build 2] At the end, half of the summers are past the 1-in-1000-year level. The population samples a tilted law, and because we know the tilt exactly, we can undo it: each final copy carries weight Z-hat times e to the minus k T A. Z-hat, the product of the mean weights, is an unbiased estimate of the normalizer. So the estimate of the probability is unbiased, at any threshold.

Methods · splitting

Splitting: climb toward a tipping point one level at a time

Run $N$ trajectories. Repeatedly kill the one that got least far (by a score $\xi$) and restart it from a survivor, at the level just reached.
$\hat p=\displaystyle\prod_{q}\Big(1-\frac{K_q}{N}\Big)\cdot\frac{\#\{\text{reached }B\}}{N}$
Unbiased for any score $\xi$; the variance is smallest when $\xi$ is the committor. Cost grows like $N\log(1/p)$, not $1/p$.
In climate: collapse of the Atlantic overturning, transitions between jet states, and, with early splitting (TEAMS), sudden rainfall and heat extremes.
Kahn & Harris 1951 · Cérou & Guyader 2007 · Bréhier et al. 2016 · TAMS: Lestang et al. 2018 · Castellana et al. 2019 · Bouchet, Rolland & Simonnet 2019 · TEAMS: Finkel & O'Gorman 2024, 2026 · toy: double well, $\varepsilon=0.04$

The second family is splitting, which goes back to Kahn and Harris in 1951 for neutron shielding. The modern adaptive version is adaptive multilevel splitting, AMS, from Cérou and Guyader in 2007.

[Animation] A toy tipping model: a double well, today's state at the bottom and a tipped state at the top, think of the Atlantic overturning collapsing. Sixteen trajectories start near today's state. At each iteration we find the one that got least far, kill it, in red, and restart it from a copy of a survivor at the level just reached, in teal. The orange line is the current level. It climbs.

[Build 1] The estimator is a product of survival fractions. It is unbiased for any choice of the score function, and the variance is smallest when the score is the committor again. The cost grows like log of one over p instead of one over p.

[Build 2] In climate this has been used for the collapse of the Atlantic overturning circulation and for transitions between jet states; and a variant called TEAMS, which splits a little before the level is crossed, handles sudden rainfall and heat extremes.

A question I always get: how can you possibly validate a 1-in-1000-year number with 80 years of data? You cannot do it directly. But you can build a chain of evidence.

First, check the moderate tail, the 10- to 50-year levels, out of sample, with proper tail-calibration diagnostics. [Build 1] Second, check the mechanism: do simulated extremes come from the same physics as observed ones: blocking, dry soils, wave trains? [Build 2] Third, check the estimator where the truth is known: perfect-model testbeds such as 5,000-year control runs or a 50,000-member ensemble. Report bias and confidence-interval coverage. [Build 3] Fourth, lean on theory: splitting and cloning estimators are provably unbiased for the simulator, and physical upper bounds give sanity checks. [Build 4] Fifth, treat every new record as an out-of-sample test, with care for the selection bias of studying an event because it happened.

[Build 5] And the honest bottom line: when the physics model runs the trajectories and AI only scores them, AI errors cost variance, not bias. [Build 6] Sampling error is now the solvable part. Model error in the tail is the frontier.

Backup: cloning (GKTL) and diffusion Monte Carlo

GKTL (Ragone et al. 2018)
$\dfrac{\dd P_k}{\dd P_0}=\dfrac{e^{\,k\int_0^{T_a}A(X_t)\dd t}}{Z(k,T_a)},\qquad W^i_n=\dfrac{e^{\,k\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}A(X_n)\dd t}}{R_i}$
$\hat\lambda(k)=\dfrac1{T_a}\sum_i\log R_i,\qquad \mathbb E_0[O]\approx\dfrac1N\sum_n O(X_n)\,e^{-k\int_0^{T_a}A(X_n)\dd t+T_a\hat\lambda}$
Typical value under $P_k$: $\lambda'(k)$. $k$ acts as an inverse temperature, $\lambda$ as a free energy (Gärtner–Ellis).
DMC (Webber et al. 2019; Lancelin et al. 2026)
$w^i_k=\bar w_{k-1}\,e^{\,V_k(X^i_k)-V_{k-1}(\hat X^i_{k-1})},\qquad \mathbb E[N^i_k]=w^i_k/\bar w_k$
$\hat p_a=\dfrac{\bar w_K}{N}\sum_i\mathbb 1[A^i>a]\,e^{-V_K(\hat X^i_K)}$
GKTL is DMC with the path-dependent potential $V_k=k\int_0^{t_k}A\,\dd t$. With $V_k=\log h_k$ (the committor) the weights are martingale increments and the final population is exactly $P(\cdot\mid E)$.
Return times from weighted trajectories (Lestang et al. 2018)
$\hat r(a)=-\dfrac{T_a}{\ln\!\big(1-\sum_m p_m\,\mathbb 1\{a_m>a\}\big)}\;\approx\;\dfrac{T_a}{\hat p(a)}\quad\text{for rare events}$

Backup: splitting and stochastic optimal control

Adaptive multilevel splitting
$\hat p=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\Big(1-\dfrac{K_j}{N}\Big)\cdot\dfrac{\#\{\text{reached }B\}}{N}$
  • unbiased for any score and any $N$ (Bréhier et al. 2016)
  • ideal score (committor): $\mathrm{Var}\,\hat p\approx -p^2\ln p/N$
  • never worse than crude Monte Carlo in the rare regime
  • fails for sudden events unless split early (TEAMS)
Control and the Doob transform
$\dfrac{\dd P}{\dd Q^u}=\exp\Big(-\!\int_0^T\! u\cdot\dd W^Q-\tfrac12\!\int_0^T\!|u|^2\dd t\Big)$
$V=-\log h,\quad V(t,x)=\min_u\mathbb E\Big[\int_t^T\big(f+\tfrac12|u|^2\big)\dd s+g\Big],\quad u^\star=\sigma^{\!\top}\nabla\log h$
$-\log\mathbb E_P[e^{-W}]=\min_Q\{\mathbb E_Q[W]+\mathrm{KL}(Q\Vert P)\}$: the optimal tilt is a KL-regularized control problem, which is what neural samplers and SOC solvers learn.
