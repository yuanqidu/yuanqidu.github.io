Why lattice field theory

Near 1 GeV the strong force has no small parameter

Every particle is a ripple in a quantum field. Predictions are averages over all field configurations, Feynman's path integral:
$\langle\mathcal O\rangle=\dfrac1Z\displaystyle\int\mathcal D\phi\;\mathcal O[\phi]\,e^{\,iS[\phi]/\hbar}$
Perturbation theory expands in a small coupling: superb for electromagnetism. The strong coupling $\alpha_s$ runs: small at high energy (asymptotic freedom, Nobel 2004), of order one near 1 GeV, exactly where protons and pions live.
Confinement
Quarks and gluons are never seen alone, only bound into hadrons.
Mass from interaction
Up and down quark masses give about 1% of the proton mass; the rest is field energy.
Chiral symmetry breaking
Why pions are so much lighter than protons.
The QCD transition
How the quark–gluon plasma of the early universe became hadrons.
We need a method without a small coupling. Lattice field theory is the only one known that is systematically improvable from first principles.

Why do we need this? Every particle is a ripple in a quantum field, and to predict anything you average over all field configurations, weighted by e to the i S. The usual approach expands in a small coupling. For electromagnetism the coupling is one over 137, and perturbation theory works spectacularly. [Animation] For the strong force, the coupling runs with the energy scale. It is small at high energy, which is asymptotic freedom, but it grows to order one near one GeV, which is exactly the scale of protons and pions. There, each order of perturbation theory is as large as the last.

[Build 1] And that is where the most basic facts of nuclear physics are decided: confinement; the fact that 99 percent of the proton mass comes from field energy, not from quark masses; chiral symmetry breaking, which makes pions light; and the QCD transition in the early universe.

[Build 2] So we need a method that does not rely on a small coupling. The lattice is the only one known that is systematically improvable from first principles.

From path integral to grid

The recipe: from a path integral to a grid, in four steps

1
Replace spacetime by a hypercubic grid $x=a\,n$, $n\in\mathbb Z^d$, with $L$ points per direction and periodic boundaries.
2
Replace the field $\phi(x)$ by one variable $\phi_n$ per site (for gauge fields, one per link).
3
Derivatives become differences, integrals become sums: $\partial_\mu\phi\to(\phi_{n+\hat\mu}-\phi_n)/a$, $\int d^dx\to a^d\sum_n$.
4
Sample configurations with probability $e^{-S}/Z$ and average over them.
For a free scalar field of mass $m$:
$S=\displaystyle\sum_n a^d\Big[\tfrac12\sum_\mu\frac{(\phi_{n+\hat\mu}-\phi_n)^2}{a^2}+\tfrac12m^2\phi_n^2\Big],\quad Z=\int\prod_n d\phi_n\,e^{-S}$

A finite grid has finitely many variables, so every quantity is computable. The spacing $a$ is scaffolding: physics is what survives $a\to0$.

The recipe has four steps, and it is the same for every model today. From path integral to grid · warm-up

Warm-up: quantum mechanics is a 1D lattice field theory

One particle in a potential $V$. Its "field" is the path $x(\tau)$. Slice Euclidean time into steps of length $a$, periodic:
$S=\displaystyle\sum_{n}\Big[\frac{m}{2a}(x_{n+1}-x_n)^2+a\,V(x_n)\Big]$
  • A configuration is a path, sampled with probability $e^{-S}/Z$.
  • Histogram of positions → ground-state density $|\psi_0(x)|^2$.
  • The correlator decays with the gap: $C(\tau)=\langle x(\tau)x(0)\rangle\to c\,e^{-(E_1-E_0)\tau}$.
  • Effective mass $m_{\rm eff}(\tau)=\tfrac1a\ln\tfrac{C(\tau)}{C(\tau+a)}$: its plateau is $E_1-E_0$.
  • Double well: the path tunnels between the wells (instantons); the gap is small (0.23 at this $a$), and local updates struggle to balance the two wells.

From path integral to grid

The continuum limit is a critical point

The lattice does not know $a$ in metres. You set dimensionless couplings and measure dimensionless numbers like $\xi/a$. A particle of mass $m$ is a correlation decaying as $e^{-r/\xi}$ with $\xi=1/m$. Fixed physics on ever finer grids means
$a\to0\ \text{at fixed}\ \xi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\xi/a\to\infty$
A diverging correlation length is a critical point. Continuum field theories live at the critical points of lattice models, and universality is why a crude grid can still give exact continuum physics.
The renormalization group makes this visible: coarse-grain and watch the picture flow. At $T_c$ every level looks alike: a fixed point.
The algorithmic sting. A local move carries information about one lattice spacing per update, so decorrelating a region of size $\xi$ takes $\sim(\xi/a)^z$ sweeps with $z\approx2$: critical slowing down.

Nothing on the lattice knows the value of a in metres. You set dimensionless couplings and measure dimensionless numbers like the correlation length in lattice units. A particle of fixed mass has a fixed correlation length, so describing it on ever finer grids means xi over a goes to infinity. A diverging correlation length is the definition of a critical point. So continuum field theories live at the critical points of lattice models, and universality is why a crude grid can still give exact continuum physics.

[Build 2] And here is the sting. A local Monte Carlo move carries information about one lattice spacing per update, so decorrelating a region of size xi takes about xi to the z sweeps, with z around two. That is critical slowing down, the central computational problem of the field.

Monte Carlo

Monte Carlo in one slide

A 100 × 100 Ising lattice has $2^{10000}$ configurations. Sample instead, and average:
$\langle\mathcal O\rangle\approx\dfrac1K\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^K\mathcal O[\phi^{(k)}],\qquad\text{error}\approx\sigma_{\mathcal O}\sqrt{2\tau_{\rm int}/K}$
A Markov chain samples $p=e^{-S}/Z$ if it is ergodic and obeys detailed balance. Metropolis (1953): propose a symmetric change, accept with
$P_{\rm acc}=\min\big(1,\,e^{-\Delta S}\big),\qquad\Delta S=S[\phi']-S[\phi]$
$Z$ never appears. Changing one site touches only its neighbours: $O(1)$ work per update, $O(V)$ per sweep.
  • Heat bath (Gibbs): draw the new value from its exact conditional; always accepted.
  • Overrelaxation: jump to the "mirror image" value with the same action; free, but not ergodic alone.
Figure of merit: $\tau_{\rm int}=\tfrac12+\sum_{t\ge1}\rho(t)$ sweeps per independent sample. Compare algorithms by cost per effective sample.

Monte Carlo in one slide. Even a 100 by 100 Ising lattice has two to the ten thousand configurations, so we sample and average, with a statistical error controlled by the integrated autocorrelation time. Lattice Monte Carlo is Markov chain Monte Carlo. Metropolis, 1953: propose a symmetric change, accept with probability min of one and e to the minus delta S. [Animation] Here is one step at a time on an eight by eight Ising model: pick a spin, add up its four neighbours, compute the energy change, compare with a uniform random number. The normalization Z never appears, and each update costs order one work.

[Build 1] Two other single-variable updates recur all talk: heat bath, which draws the new value from its exact conditional and is always accepted; and overrelaxation, which jumps to the mirror image with the same action: a free move, but not ergodic on its own.

[Build 2] The figure of merit is the autocorrelation time: sweeps per independent sample. We compare algorithms by cost per effective sample. If you come from ML, this is the MCMC you know; what is special here is locality, huge symmetry groups, and the demand for exact answers.

Spin models · Ising

The Ising model: the hydrogen atom of statistical physics

A spin $s_i=\pm1$ on each site; neighbours like to align:
$H=-J\displaystyle\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j-h\sum_is_i,\qquad p(s)=\frac{e^{-H/T}}{Z}$
At $h=0$, flipping every spin is a symmetry ($\mathbb Z_2$). Below $T_c$ it is spontaneously broken: $|m|>0$.
Exactly solvable in 2D (Onsager 1944; Yang 1952):
$T_c=\dfrac{2}{\ln(1+\sqrt2)}\approx2.269,\quad |m|=\big(1-\sinh^{-4}\tfrac2T\big)^{1/8}$
Near $T_c$: power laws, $|m|\propto(T_c-T)^{1/8}$ and $\xi\propto|T-T_c|^{-1}$. These critical exponents are universal. In 3D there is no exact solution; Monte Carlo and the conformal bootstrap give $\nu\approx0.630$.

Spin models · the central problem

Critical slowing down: local updates cannot keep up

To change the magnetization of a correlated region of size $\xi$, information must diffuse across it:
$\tau\propto\xi^{\,z}\ \ \longrightarrow\ \ \tau\propto L^{z}\ \text{at}\ T_c$
For Metropolis and heat bath in the 2D Ising model, the dynamical exponent is $z\approx2.17$.
In field theory it strikes as $a\to0$: halving $a$ doubles $\xi/a$, and multiplies the autocorrelation time by $2^z\approx4.5$ on top of $2^d$ times more sites.
Doubling $L$ makes Metropolis ≈ 4.5 × slower per independent sample; Wolff hardly changes ($z\approx0.25$).

Now the central problem. Local algorithms change one spin at a time, so changing the magnetization of a correlated region requires information to diffuse across it. The autocorrelation time grows like xi to the z, and at T_c on a finite lattice, like L to the z. For Metropolis in 2D Ising, z is about 2.17. In field theory this strikes as a goes to zero: halving the spacing multiplies the autocorrelation time by about 4.5, on top of 2 to the d more sites. Spin models · clusters

Cluster algorithms: flip whole correlated regions

Fortuin–Kasteleyn (1972): connect equal neighbours with probability
$p=1-e^{-2J/T}$
and the clusters can be flipped independently. Swendsen–Wang (1987) flips all of them; Wolff (1989) grows and flips one, from a random seed. This $p$ makes detailed balance exact, so no move is ever rejected.

  • At $T_c$ clusters are fractal and come in all sizes, so one flip changes every length scale: $z$ drops from 2.17 to about 0.25.

Spin models · lessons

Two bugs worth remembering (caught while testing the demos)

Measure at times that do not depend on the state
A natural Wolff "sweep": flip clusters until $N$ spins have flipped, then measure. Each move is exact, but big clusters end the sweep early, so the moment of measurement depends on the configuration.
2D Ising at $T=3$: energy −0.8245 instead of the exact −0.8173, about 20 standard errors. Caught because XY energies from Wolff disagreed with three local algorithms.
Fix: a fixed number of clusters per sweep, tuned in warm-up, then frozen.
Fixed trajectory lengths can resonate
HMC on a free field: $\langle\phi^2\rangle$ drifted 4σ away from the exact value. A fixed trajectory length can resonate with a mode of the field and stop it from decorrelating.
Fix: jitter the step size randomly from trajectory to trajectory (Mackenzie 1989).
The general lesson, familiar from adaptive MCMC: anything you adapt must stop adapting, or must not depend on the current state, before you measure. And always test against an exact answer.

A short detour with two bugs we caught while building the demos behind this talk, because they generalize. The first: a natural way to define a Wolff sweep is to keep flipping clusters until N spins have flipped, then measure. Every cluster move is exact, but big clusters end the sweep early, so the moment of measurement depends on the configuration. That biased the energy of the 2D Ising model at T equals 3 by about 20 standard errors. The fix is a fixed number of clusters per sweep, tuned in warm-up and then frozen.

[Build 1] The second: HMC on a free field drifted four sigma away from the exact value of phi squared. A fixed trajectory length resonated with a mode of the field. Jittering the step size randomly from trajectory to trajectory cured it, a trick from Mackenzie in 1989.

[Build 2] The lesson is familiar from adaptive MCMC: anything you adapt must stop adapting, or must not depend on the current state, before you measure. And always test against an exact answer; that is how both were caught.

Spin models · Potts

The Potts model: more colours, and first-order transitions

$H=-J\displaystyle\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\delta_{s_i,s_j},\quad s_i\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}$
$q=2$ is Ising. In 2D, duality gives $\beta_c=\ln(1+\sqrt q)$ exactly. For $q\le4$ the transition is continuous; for $q>4$ it is first order (Baxter 1973): two phases coexist at $\beta_c$.
To switch phase, a finite box must build two interfaces: rate $\propto e^{-2\sigma L^{d-1}}$. Supercritical slowing down, exponential in $L$; no local or cluster move fixes it.
Cure: change the ensemble, then reweight. Multicanonical (Berg & Neuhaus 1991), Wang–Landau (2001), parallel tempering (1986, 1996).
Why QCD cares: near deconfinement, SU(3) gauge theory behaves like the 3D 3-state Potts model (Svetitsky & Yaffe 1982), and indeed deconfines through a first-order transition.
Live Swendsen–Wang updates at $\beta_c$ · dashed: exact energies of the two coexisting phases (Baxter)

Replace the two spin values by q colours, with an energy reward for equal neighbours. For q equals 2 it is Ising again. In two dimensions duality gives the critical point exactly. What changes with q is the nature of the transition: continuous for q up to 4, first order above 4, as Baxter showed. [Animation] Here is q equals 3 at the critical point: domains of all sizes, and the order parameter cloud has a three-fold shape. Remember that shape.

Spin models · updates

Three ways to update an angle

Metropolis
Random kick $\theta'=\theta+\delta$, accept with $\min(1,e^{-\Delta E/T})$. Simple and general; small steps diffuse slowly, big ones get rejected.
Heat bath
Draw directly from the conditional, a von Mises law $p(\theta)\propto e^{\beta\,\mathbf h\cdot\mathbf s(\theta)}$ with local field $\mathbf h=\sum_j\mathbf s_j$. Always accepted.
Overrelaxation
Reflect about $\mathbf h$: $\theta'=2\varphi_h-\theta$. Energy unchanged, so free and always accepted; not ergodic alone.
Heat bath plus a few overrelaxation sweeps is still the standard recipe for pure gauge theories (Adler 1981; Creutz 1987; Brown & Woch 1987).

With continuous variables there are three natural local updates, and good algorithms combine them. [Animation] One XY spin with four fixed neighbours; on the right, its conditional distribution. Metropolis: a random kick, accepted or rejected; small steps diffuse slowly and large ones get rejected. Heat bath: draw directly from the conditional, a von Mises distribution around the local field; always accepted. Overrelaxation: reflect the spin about the local field. The energy is exactly unchanged, so the move is free and always accepted, and it carries the spin as far as possible; but it never changes the energy, so on its own it is not ergodic.

For gauge theories, a heat-bath sweep followed by a few overrelaxation sweeps is still the standard recipe.

Fields · free field

The free field: mass = 1/ξ, and sampling is an exact flow

Gauge fields · U(1)

Local symmetry needs variables on the links

Rotate a complex matter field by a different phase at every site, $\varphi_x\to e^{i\Lambda_x}\varphi_x$: the hopping term $\varphi_x^*\varphi_{x+\hat\mu}$ is no longer invariant. Comparing phases at two points needs a parallel transporter on the link:
$U_{x,\mu}=e^{i\theta_{x,\mu}}\to e^{i\Lambda_x}U_{x,\mu}e^{-i\Lambda_{x+\hat\mu}},\quad U_{x,\mu}\approx e^{iaA_\mu(x)}$
The smallest invariant: the plaquette, the product of links around a square. Its angle is the magnetic flux, $\theta_P\approx a^2F_{\mu\nu}$. Wilson's action:
$S=\beta\displaystyle\sum_P\big(1-\cos\theta_P\big)\ \xrightarrow{a\to0}\ \frac1{4e^2}\int F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}$
Only closed loops are physical
A configuration and all its gauge transforms have the same weight; only gauge-invariant observables can be nonzero (Elitzur).
Compact angles bring topology
Keeping phases rather than $A_\mu$ keeps gauge invariance exact, and makes the action periodic: topology, and monopoles in 4D.

So far symmetries were global. The forces of nature come from local symmetries. Take a complex matter field and demand invariance under a different phase rotation at every site. The hopping term is no longer invariant: comparing phases at two points has become meaningless, unless we add a new variable that says how to carry a phase from one site to its neighbour. That is the link variable, a phase on each link, and in the continuum limit it is e to the i a times the vector potential. The covariant hop does not, and neither does the plaquette angle in each square.

[Build 1] The plaquette, the product of links around a square, is the smallest gauge-invariant object, and its angle is the magnetic flux through the square. Wilson's action charges a cost for every plaquette, and it becomes Maxwell's action as a goes to zero.

[Build 2] Two consequences: only closed loops are physical; and because Wilson kept compact phases, the action is periodic, which brings topology.

Gauge fields · confinement

Wilson loops and confinement

A static charge and anticharge at distance $R$, living for Euclidean time $T$: their world-lines form a rectangle, and the gauge field's response is the product of links around it.
$W(R,T)=\Big\langle\displaystyle\prod_{\ell\in\partial(R\times T)}U_\ell\Big\rangle\propto e^{-V(R)\,T}$. Area law $W\sim e^{-\sigma RT}$ ⇒ $V(R)=\sigma R$: confinement, with string tension $\sigma$.

A short Euclidean time direction is a high temperature, $T=1/(aN_t)$. The order parameter of confinement is the trace of the time links around the time circle:

Confined: an isolated quark costs infinite free energy, $\langle P\rangle=0$. Deconfined (quark–gluon plasma): $\langle P\rangle\ne0$.

QCD · quarks

Adding quarks: determinants and doublers

Quarks are anticommuting Grassmann variables, which a computer cannot sample. The action is quadratic in them, so integrate them out exactly:
$Z=\displaystyle\int\mathcal DU\,\big(\det D[U]\big)^{N_f}e^{-S_g[U]},\quad \det(D^\dagger D)\propto\int\mathcal D\phi^\dagger\mathcal D\phi\,e^{-\phi^\dagger(D^\dagger D)^{-1}\phi}$
The determinant couples all links. Pseudofermions $\phi$ turn it into a Gaussian integral that HMC can handle, at the price of a huge sparse solve $(D^\dagger D)\chi=\phi$ per step: most of the world's lattice-QCD computing goes into conjugate gradient, deflation and multigrid solvers.

Finally, quarks. They are fermions, described by anticommuting Grassmann variables that a computer cannot sample. But the action is quadratic in them, so they can be integrated out exactly, leaving a determinant of the lattice Dirac operator. For two light flavours the weight is still positive. But the determinant couples all the links, so the standard trick writes it as a Gaussian integral over bosonic pseudofermion fields, and samples everything with HMC. Every molecular-dynamics step then needs the solution of a huge sparse linear system. That is where most of the world's lattice-QCD computing time goes.

Here is what each family is liked for and where it struggles. Local chains: universal, exact, trivially parallel, but critical slowing down and freezing. Clusters: nearly remove critical slowing down, but are model-specific and do not exist for gauge theories with quarks. Molecular dynamics: global gradient-guided moves and the only practical route with dynamical quarks, but freezing on fine lattices and expensive solves. Normalizing flows: exact densities, independent samples

An invertible map from noise to fields, $\phi=T(z)$, $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, built from layers with cheap Jacobians, so the model density is exact:
$\log q(\phi)=\log\mathcal N(z)-\log\Big|\det\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial z}\Big|$
Train by the reverse Kullback–Leibler divergence. It needs only samples from $q$ and the action: no training data.
$\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p)=\mathbb E_{\phi\sim q}\big[\log q(\phi)+S(\phi)\big]+\log Z$
$q$ is never exactly $p$, so correct it, exactly:

Either use the flow as the proposal of an independence Metropolis–Hastings chain, as Albergo, Kanwar and Shanahan did in 2019; or give each sample the importance weight e to the minus S over q, whose average estimates the partition function, and with it the free energy.

The sign problem: when $e^{-S}$ is not a probability

The Euclidean weight is complex for QCD at nonzero baryon density (neutron stars), for real-time dynamics, and for many fermion systems (the Hubbard model away from half filling). Sample $|e^{-S}|$ and move the phase into the observable:
$\langle\mathcal O\rangle=\dfrac{\langle\mathcal O\,e^{i\theta}\rangle_{|\cdot|}}{\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle_{|\cdot|}},\qquad\langle e^{i\theta}\rangle_{|\cdot|}=\dfrac{Z}{Z_{|\cdot|}}=e^{-V\Delta f}$
The average sign decays exponentially with volume; the noise shrinks only like $1/\sqrt N$. NP-hard in general (Troyer & Wiese 2005): no universal fix is expected.

Backup: why clusters need no accept/reject, and the φ⁴ dictionary

Wolff's bond probability
Flip a cluster $C$. On its boundary, $n_{\rm same}$ pairs had equal spins before the flip, $n_{\rm diff}$ unequal. Building exactly $C$ required rejecting each of the $n_{\rm same}$ boundary bonds; the reverse move swaps the roles:
$\dfrac{T(\text{fwd})}{T(\text{rev})}=(1-p)^{\,n_{\rm same}-n_{\rm diff}},\qquad\Delta E=2J\,(n_{\rm same}-n_{\rm diff})$
Detailed balance needs this ratio to equal $e^{-\Delta E/T}$ for every cluster: exactly when $1-p=e^{-2J/T}$.
Continuum ↔ lattice φ⁴
Discretize $\partial_\mu\phi\to(\phi_{x+\hat\mu}-\phi_x)/a$ and expand the square: the cross terms become the hopping term with coefficient $\kappa$. Matching the two forms:
$a^{(d-2)/2}\phi_{\rm cont}=\sqrt{2\kappa}\,\phi,\quad (am_0)^2=\dfrac{1-2\lambda}{\kappa}-2d,\quad a^{4-d}\lambda_0=\dfrac{6\lambda}{\kappa^2}$
